If you've played more than one Paradox grand strategy game, you've probably noticed they feel similar under the hood. That's not a coincidence — they all run on the Clausewitz engine, Paradox's in-house game engine. Here's what that means for how these games play, how they mod, and what to expect from future Paradox titles.

## What Is the Clausewitz Engine?

Clausewitz is Paradox's proprietary game engine, named after the Prussian military theorist Carl von Clausewitz. It's been in development since the late 2000s and has powered every major Paradox grand strategy game since — EU4, CK3, EU5, and others like Hearts of Iron and Victoria.

The engine is designed specifically for grand strategy games. It handles the map, the province system, the character system, the event system, and the modding tools that make Paradox games so customizable.

## What the Engine Means for Gameplay

Because EU4, EU5, and CK3 share an engine, they share certain fundamental characteristics:

**The map.** All three games use a province-based map. The world is divided into provinces, and each province has its own characteristics — development, terrain, resources, and so on. This is the foundation of all Paradox grand strategy.

**The event system.** All three games use a scripted event system. Events fire based on conditions, and your choices have consequences. This is how Paradox delivers content — through events, decisions, and missions.

**The modding tools.** This is the big one. The Clausewitz engine is designed to be moddable. The game data is stored in text files that modders can edit, and the engine supports custom content, new mechanics, and even total conversions. This is why EU4 and CK3 have such massive modding communities.

**The performance profile.** The engine is known for being CPU-heavy, especially in the late game. As your empire grows and the number of provinces, characters, and events increases, the game slows down. This is a known characteristic of all Clausewitz games.

## What the Engine Means for Modding

The Clausewitz engine's moddability is one of its greatest strengths. Here's what it enables:

**Total conversions.** Mods like Anbennar (EU4) and the various CK3 total conversions completely replace the game's content — new maps, new mechanics, new events. The engine supports this level of modification.

**Mechanic mods.** Mods that add or change mechanics — new government types, new succession laws, new trade systems. The engine's scripting language makes this possible.

**Content mods.** New events, decisions, and flavor. This is the easiest type of mod to make, and it's how most modders start.

The downside is that the engine's scripting language has a learning curve. It's not as accessible as some modern engines, but the community has built extensive documentation and tools over the years.

## EU5 and the Engine

EU5 runs on an updated version of the Clausewitz engine. Paradox has made significant improvements — better performance, improved graphics, and new systems like the pop system that weren't possible in the older engine versions.

The key question for EU5 is whether the modding community will be able to do the same things they did with EU4. The engine is more modern, which should mean more capability, but it also means modders need to learn new tools and systems. It'll take time, but the foundation is there.

## The Bottom Line

The Clausewitz engine is the reason Paradox games feel like Paradox games. It's the shared foundation that makes EU4, EU5, and CK3 feel related — the map, the events, the modding, the performance quirks. It's also the reason these games have such thriving modding communities.

Understanding the engine helps you understand the games. When you know that the map is province-based, that events are scripted, and that modding is supported, you understand why these games play the way they do. And when EU5's modding scene matures, it'll be because the engine supports it.

The Clausewitz engine has been Paradox's workhorse for over a decade, and it's not going anywhere. It's the foundation of the grand strategy genre.
